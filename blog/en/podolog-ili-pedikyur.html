Podiatry or pedicure: what the difference is and when you need a specialist

August 4, 2026·Reading ~7 min·Podiatry

A foot care specialist is what you need where an ordinary pedicure stops being enough: an ingrown nail, deep heel fissures, calluses that come back every month. A pedicure is about how feet look; podiatry is about how they are and how they work. Here is where the line falls. BOSCO's current prices are on the podiatry page.

What a foot care specialist does

The work starts where ordinary cosmetic treatment is ineffective or outright risky.

  • Ingrown nails, and preventing them from recurring.
  • Deep fissures, severe dryness, thickened skin.
  • Calluses, including the kind with a hard core.
  • Nails thickened or deformed after an injury.
  • Prevention where feet take heavy load.

An important clarification, and one that trips up newcomers: in most countries this specialist is not a physician. They do not diagnose and do not prescribe. Their remit is mechanical work on the problem and stopping it returning. Where infection, inflammation or a systemic condition is suspected, they refer you on.

Ingrown nails: why they happen

This is the single most common reason for a first appointment. The mechanism is simple: the edge of the nail plate presses into the side fold, the skin responds with inflammation, and the pain makes people cut the corner deeper still — which closes the loop.

What causes it:

  • The habit of cutting nails round, digging into the corners.
  • Narrow or hard-toed shoes.
  • Heavy load on the feet: running, long hours standing.
  • The natural shape of the nail plate.

What the specialist does: carefully frees the nail edge from pressure, treats the fold, and where needed fits a correction brace — a thin plate that gently turns the nail into the right shape over several months. No cutting is involved.

If the skin around the fold is red, swollen or discharging, that is inflammation. In that case what you need is a doctor, not a salon appointment.

Heel fissures and dryness

Tashkent's climate works openly against you here: dry air and summer heat pull moisture out of the skin, and open shoes leave the heel edge unprotected. The skin thickens, then splits.

Surface dryness can be handled at home: a moisturiser used consistently, gentle treatment rather than a pumice stone. The key is not to remove a lot of hard skin at once — the body answers by building a thicker layer.

If the fissures are deep, hurt when you walk or bleed, you need a specialist. They remove the overhanging edges in careful layers, treat the base of the fissure and select aftercare so it closes rather than reopening a week later.

Calluses and corns

A plain callus is the skin's answer to pressure. It forms where there is constant friction and disappears on its own once the cause is removed.

A corn with a hard core behaves differently: it has a dense centre that presses inward and hurts with every step. Removing it at the surface does not help — the core stays and the corn returns.

The specialist takes the core out with an e-file and, more importantly, works out the cause: shoes, gait, a change in toe shape. Otherwise the same thing recurs in the same spot within a couple of months.

When to see a doctor instead

The rule of thumb: a foot care specialist deals with mechanics, a doctor deals with disease.

Specialist: an ingrown nail without inflammation, calluses and corns, dryness and fissures, nails thickened after injury, prevention under load.

Doctor first:

  • Redness, swelling, discharge or warmth around the nail.
  • Suspected fungal infection: several nails discoloured, crumbling or thickened. A dermatologist diagnoses that from a sample, not a technician by eye.
  • Diabetes, reduced sensation or circulatory problems — here any treatment must be agreed with the doctor managing your care.
  • Wounds on the foot that are not healing.
  • Moles or new growths on the sole or toes.

One further boundary: this is not orthopaedic care. If calluses keep forming in exactly the same place, the cause often lies in your gait or the shape of your foot — a different specialist's territory. A good practitioner spots that and refers you.

What an appointment looks like

  1. Assessment. The specialist looks at the whole foot: nails, skin, load points, the state of your shoes.
  2. Conversation. What is bothering you, when it started, what footwear you wear.
  3. Treatment. Dry e-file technique, no soaking bowls — more hygienic, and the result lasts longer.
  4. Solution. Where needed, a correction brace or a protective device is fitted.
  5. Advice. What to do at home and when to come back.

An appointment usually runs an hour to an hour and a half — longer than a pedicure, because most of the time goes on identifying the cause rather than on the treatment itself.

Frequently asked questions

How is this different from a pedicure technician?

A pedicure technician works on appearance: nail work, hard skin, polish. A foot care specialist works on problems — ingrown nails, fissures, calluses. The training, the tools and the sterile protocol are different and closer to clinical practice.

Does treating an ingrown nail hurt?

E-file treatment is usually painless: the specialist relieves the pressure on the fold without cutting. Discomfort is possible if the nail is already inflamed, in which case the inflammation has to be dealt with first, and that is a question for a doctor.

How often should I go?

Once the problem is resolved, every six to eight weeks to maintain the result. If you are wearing a correction brace, the specialist sets the interval, usually four to six weeks. For prevention with no complaints, twice a year is enough.

Can I go if I have diabetes?

Yes, and specifically to a foot care specialist rather than an ordinary pedicure technician — with diabetes skin heals more slowly and any small injury is risky. Always mention the diagnosis before treatment begins, and keep your doctor's supervision in place: salon treatment does not replace medical care.

Can a specialist help with nail fungus?

They can carefully treat and thin an affected nail so that medication works better, but they do not treat the infection itself. A dermatologist makes the diagnosis and prescribes the therapy.
